## 1. The Box

Outside size: **6" L × 4" W × 3½" H**. No lid, no decoration. Stackable with a plug on the bottom.

**Stock:** 1x4 poplar, S4S (surfaced four sides), actual size ¾" × 3½". The board's width becomes the box height, so no ripping is needed. Plus ¼" plywood for the bottom and plug.

### Cut list

| Part        | Qty | Size                     | Notes                                                                                        |
| ----------- | --- | ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Long sides  | 2   | ¾" × 3½" × 6"            |                                                                                              |
| Short sides | 2   | ¾" × 3½" × 2½"           | 4" outside width minus 2 × ¾"                                                                |
| Bottom      | 1   | ¼" ply × 6" × 4"         | Glued flush to the bottom edges of the sides                                                 |
| Plug        | 1   | ¼" ply × 4⁷⁄₁₆" × 2⁷⁄₁₆" | Glued centered under the bottom. Inside opening is 4½" × 2½", so this leaves 1/16" clearance |

### How the pieces fit

- The short sides sit **between** the long sides (butt joints).
- Inside opening of the box: 4½" × 2½".
- The plug on the underside of one box drops into the opening of the box below it. That's what makes the boxes stackable.
- The plug is only ¼" tall, so it locates the stack but won't resist much sideways force. If stacks feel loose, make the plug from ½" scrap instead.

### How much to buy per box

Parts total 17" of 1x4, and the saw kerfs and trimmed ends use up more, so plan on about 20" per box. A 1x4x4' board (48") makes two boxes with margin for mistakes. Buy extra: the first boxes will have mistakes.

## 2. Shopping List

**Wood**

- 1x4 poplar, S4S, several 4' or 6' lengths (cheap, easy to work, glues well, forgiving to saw and plane)
- ¼" plywood, a 2'×2' or 2'×4' sheet (makes bottoms and plugs for many boxes)
- Pine is a fine, cheaper substitute while you're learning

**Supplies**

- PVA wood glue (Titebond I or II)
- Sandpaper: 120 and 180 grit, plus a sanding block
- Finish: boiled linseed oil, Danish oil, or paste wax
- Painter's tape (can clamp small glue-ups) and a rag
- Pencil, and a few sacrificial scrap boards

---

## 3. Tools

**Marking and measuring**

- Steel rule or tape measure
- Combination square or speed square (check that it's square: draw a line, flip the square, and draw it again)
- Marking knife (a utility knife works)
- Marking gauge (needed once you move to rabbets)
- Pencil

**Cutting**

- Circular saw (fine-tooth blade, 40 teeth or more)
- Backsaw or dovetail saw (for hand cuts and cleanup)
- Chisel, ¾" or 1" (for cleanup and later rabbets)

**Shaping and smoothing**

- Block plane (or a low-angle block plane)
- Sanding block

**Holding**

- Two or more clamps (F-clamps or bar clamps) to hold stock for sawing
- Band clamp, or a few clamps plus tape, for glue-ups
- Workbench or sturdy table with a vise or a way to clamp stock

**Safety**

- Safety glasses, hearing protection, dust mask when sanding

## 4. Safety

- Wear eye and ear protection every time the circular saw runs.
- Keep both hands behind the blade path. Never hold the offcut or reach across the blade.
- **Clamp the stock.** Don't hold small pieces by hand while a saw runs against them. The 2½" short sides are too small to hold safely, so cut them from a longer blank (see section 6).
- Let the blade reach full speed before it touches wood, and wait for it to stop completely before lifting the saw or setting it down.
- Check the blade guard moves freely and snaps back.
- Unplug the saw before changing blades or adjusting depth.
- Keep the power cord out of the cut path.
- Sharp tools are safer than dull ones. A dull chisel or plane makes you push harder, and that's how slips happen. Always keep both hands, and your body, out of the path the chisel travels.
- Work in good light. You can't hit a line you can't see.

## 5. Glossary

**Proud:** sticking out past a surface or line. "Cut just proud of the line" means leave the piece a hair too long, then trim it back to the line.

**Shy:** the opposite of proud. A piece cut shy is too short, and it can't be fixed by trimming.

**Waste side:** the side of the line that gets thrown away. Saw on this side so the kept piece stays full size and the line stays visible.

**Kerf:** the slot the saw blade removes, about 1/8" wide for a circular saw. Account for it when you measure.

**Knife line:** a line scored with a knife rather than drawn with a pencil. It's thinner and more accurate, and it gives the saw or chisel a groove to register against.

**Sneak up on the line:** make cuts slightly proud, then remove material in small steps until you're exactly at the line.

**Reference face / reference edge:** the one face and one edge you treat as the flat, square starting point. Every mark is measured from them, so errors don't pile up.

**S4S:** surfaced on four sides. The lumber is already flat and square, and sold at nominal sizes (a "1x4" is really ¾" × 3½").

**Grain:** the direction the wood fibers run. **Long grain** is along the board's length. **End grain** is the cut end.

**Tearout:** splintered wood fibers torn out at the edge of a cut or while planing, often where the blade exits or when planing against the grain.

**Butt joint:** a joint where one piece's end meets another piece's face, with no interlocking cut.

**Rabbet:** a step cut along the edge or end of a board, so another piece can sit in it.

**Dovetail:** an interlocking joint of flared pins and tails. Very strong, and the classic hand-cut skill.

**Dry fit:** assembling the parts without glue to check the fit.

**Glue-up:** the glue and clamping stage.

**Squeeze-out:** glue pushed out of a joint when clamped. A small amount is a sign you used enough.

**Racking:** a box or frame being pushed out of square, into a parallelogram.

**Diagonals:** the corner-to-corner measurements. If both are equal, the box is square.

**Flush:** level with the neighboring surface.

**Shooting board:** a jig that holds a piece so a plane, running on its side, trims the end perfectly square.

**Bench hook:** a jig that hooks over your bench edge and holds stock for crosscutting with a hand saw.

**Stop block:** a block clamped in place so every piece cut or planed lands at the same length.

**Jig:** any shop-made guide or holder that makes a task repeatable.

**Base plate (shoe):** the flat metal plate on the bottom of the circular saw that rides on the wood.

**Offset:** the distance from the blade to the edge of the base plate. You need it to place a saw guide.

**TPI / tooth count:** teeth per inch (hand saws) or total teeth (circular saw blades). More teeth gives a finer, slower cut with less tearout.

**Ply (plywood):** thin layers of wood glued with alternating grain directions. It's flat and stable, so it's good for bottoms.

**Poplar:** an inexpensive, even-grained hardwood. A standard choice for learning.

**PVA glue:** the common yellow or white wood glue.

**Grit:** sandpaper coarseness. Lower numbers are coarser (120 removes wood, 180 smooths).

## 6. Circular Saw Setup and Technique

A circular saw is quick for crosscuts, but it needs a guide to cut straight, and the cut face is too rough to glue. Treat it as the roughing tool and the plane as the finishing tool.

### Blade

- Use a fine-tooth blade (40T or more). It leaves a smoother cut and less tearout in poplar.
- Set the blade depth so it extends about ¼" below the stock. Deeper increases kickback risk and exposes more blade.

### Find your offset (do this once, and again if the blade changes)

1. Make a test cut in scrap with the saw riding freehand along a clamped straight board.
2. Measure from the cut edge to the edge of the base plate that touched the guide.
3. That distance is your offset. Write it on masking tape and stick it on the saw.
4. To cut at a line, place the guide's edge at (line + offset) on the side where the saw rides.

### Make a simple saw guide

- Clamp a speed square or a straight, square-edged board to the stock so the saw's base plate rides along it.
- Or build a crosscut guide: a straight fence screwed square to a thin plywood base. The saw runs along the fence, and the base's edge shows exactly where the blade will cut. This is the best first jig for this project.

### Cutting steps

1. Knife and pencil your line on the stock, with an X marking the waste side.
2. Set the guide using your offset so the blade cuts on the **waste side** of the line, with about 1/16" left proud.
3. Clamp the stock to the bench with the cut overhanging the edge, so the offcut falls free and doesn't pinch the blade.
4. Clamp the guide, check the offset again, and let the saw reach full speed.
5. Push through slowly and steadily, keeping the base plate pressed against the guide.
6. Release the trigger, wait for the blade to stop, then lift the saw.

### Cutting small parts safely

- The 2½" short sides are too small to hold or clamp safely against a running saw.
- Cut the short sides from a **long blank**: clamp the long board down, with the 2½" piece's cut line near the edge of the bench, and make the cut with the guide. The clamped, long end stays under control, and the small piece is the one that falls free.
- Cut the long sides (6") from the board first, and cut the short sides from what remains.

### Cleaning up

- Plane or shoot each end back to the knife line on the shooting board. Don't glue circular-saw cuts as they come off the saw.
- If you don't have a shooting board yet, clamp a sanding block's worth of 120 grit to a flat surface and sand the end square. This is slow, so build the shooting board early.

## 7. Build Process, Step by Step

1. **Choose the best faces.** Pick the best-looking face for the outside, and mark a reference face and reference edge on each piece.
2. **Mark out.** Knife a line all the way around each piece with a square. Mark lengths from the cut list, and mark the waste side with an X.
3. **Rough cut.** Cut with the circular saw (section 6), about 1/16" proud of every line. Cut long sides first, then short sides from a longer blank.
4. **True the ends.** Use the shooting board and plane to take each end to its knife line, dead square. Check with the square. Aim to have the two long sides identical in length, and the two short sides identical, because pieces that match are what make a box square.
5. **Dry fit.** Assemble the four sides without glue. Check for gaps, out-of-square corners, and a mismatch in height.
6. **Glue the sides.** Put glue on the ends of the short sides, fit them between the long sides, and clamp (band clamp, or clamps plus tape). Wipe off squeeze-out with a damp rag.
7. **Check square.** Measure the diagonals. If they differ, nudge the box with the clamp in the direction that shortens the longer diagonal, until they match.
8. **Let it dry.** About 30 minutes before unclamping, and a few hours before stressing it. Check the glue's label.
9. **Bottom.** Glue the 6" × 4" plywood to the bottom edges, flush with the outside, and clamp or weight it. Trim any overhang flush with a plane or sanding block.
10. **Plug.** Dry fit the plug into the opening of another box, and check there's about 1/16" of clearance all around. Then glue it centered on the underside of the bottom. Mark center lines on both pieces first.
11. **Sand.** 120 grit to flatten and remove marks, then 180 grit to smooth. Ease (slightly round) the sharp edges with a light pass of sandpaper.
12. **Finish.** Wipe on oil or paste wax, let it soak a few minutes, then wipe off the excess. Follow the product's directions, and be careful with oily rags (spread them flat to dry or soak them in water before throwing them away).
13. **Label.** Pencil the box number on the bottom, and log it in section 13.

**Optional reinforcement:** an end-grain butt joint is the weakest joint in this plan. Small brad nails or pins through the long sides into the short sides' ends add strength while the glue cures. The plywood bottom also stiffens the box a lot.

## 8. Scoring Each Box

Score each box 1-5 and note which items to work on next.

- [ ] Pieces match their cut-list lengths
- [ ] Matching pieces are identical in length
- [ ] Ends square (check with a square)
- [ ] Corner joints tight, no visible gaps
- [ ] Box sits flat, no rocking
- [ ] Diagonals equal
- [ ] Clean glue squeeze-out
- [ ] Plug fits the box below: drops in, no wobble, no binding
- [ ] Surface smooth, no tearout or saw marks
- [ ] Time taken: ____

## 9. Troubleshooting

| Problem                              | Likely cause                                 | Fix next time                                                        |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Gaps at the corners                  | Ends not square, or pieces of unequal length | Spend more time on the shooting board. Check ends with the square.   |
| Box is a parallelogram               | Clamped out of square                        | Measure the diagonals during glue-up and adjust the clamping.        |
| Pieces don't match in length         | Measured each separately                     | Use a stop block, or cut and trim matching pieces together.          |
| Saw cut wandered                     | No guide, or the guide moved                 | Use the guide, clamp it, and push slowly.                            |
| Cut is on the wrong side of the line | Forgot the offset, or the kerf               | Re-measure the offset. Mark the waste side with an X.                |
| Splintering (tearout) at the cut     | Dull or coarse blade, or no support          | Use a fine-tooth blade, and put painter's tape over the cut line.    |
| Plug binds in the box below          | Too big, or off-center                       | Re-measure. Sand it down, and center it with marks.                  |
| Stack is wobbly                      | Plug is too small, or too short              | Make it closer to 1/32" clearance, or use ½" stock.                  |
| Box rocks on the bench               | Bottom or edges not flat                     | Plane or sand the bottom flat on a flat surface.                     |
| Glue shows through the finish        | Squeeze-out left to dry                      | Wipe it off with a damp rag right away, since dried glue blocks oil. |

## 10. Jigs to Build

Build these early. They pay for themselves by about box 10.

1. **Crosscut guide for the circular saw:** a straight fence screwed square to a thin plywood base. The saw runs along the fence, and the base's edge shows where the blade cuts.
2. **Bench hook:** a flat board with a cleat on the underside that hooks the bench edge, and a cleat on top as a stop for holding stock against while you saw by hand.
3. **Shooting board:** a base board, a raised ledge that the plane rides on its side, and a stop that holds the work against it. Used to square and trim ends.
4. **Stop block:** a scrap block clamped to the guide or bench so every piece comes out the same length.

## 11. Progression

1. **Boxes 1-10:** butt joints. Focus on square cuts, matching pieces, and tight glue-ups.
2. **Boxes 11-25:** rabbet joints (marking gauge, backsaw, chisel cleanup). Replace the butt joints on the corners.
3. **Boxes 26+:** one set of hand-cut dovetails per box.
4. **Later:** stack notches or tabs instead of the plug, and add a lid (saw it off a closed box so the grain matches).

Change one thing at a time. A new joint plus a new tool plus new wood at the same time hides what caused the problem.

---

## 12. Rules for the Practice

- Change **one** variable per box (a new tool, a tighter tolerance, a new joint).
- Keep every box, and number it. Compare #1 with #20.
- Use cheap wood. Mistakes should cost nothing.
- Aim for the same box, a little better each time, not a perfect one.
- Check every box against a square and a straightedge. A gap you can see is the feedback.
- If a box fails, finish it anyway. The sanding and finishing steps are practice too.
